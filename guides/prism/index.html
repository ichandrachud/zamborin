Prism strategy: how to split the light and fill every gem

Prism hides nothing. Every beam is drawn, every gem shows what it wants, and the whole board relights the moment you flip a mirror. So solving one is never about guessing. It is about deciding which beam to fix first, and knowing when fixing one will cost you another.

Get the white beam into the prism before anything else

Nothing on the board can be right until the white beam reaches the prism, because until it does there is no red, green or blue to route anywhere, and the board will show you exactly that: one white line and three gems sitting in the dark. The white beam is the brightest thing on the board and it comes from a single lamp, so it is easy to follow. Trace it from the lamp and find the first mirror that sends it somewhere useless. That mirror is your first move, every time. And note that reaching the prism is not enough on its own: it has to arrive on the flat face, which is the next section.

The prism has a front, and only the front works

This is the rule that catches everyone first. A prism is a solid wedge of glass with a flat face and an apex, and it only does anything to light that arrives square on the flat face. Light that strikes one of its slanted sides is scattered and lost, exactly as it would be in real glass. So the prism is not a passive splitter you can feed from any direction. It is a piece with a correct side, and finding the route that approaches it correctly is usually the spine of the whole puzzle.

You can see which face is the working one: it glows harder than the other two, and the apex points away from it. Red will leave through that apex.

Know which way each colour leaves

Light entering the flat face head on does not bend at all. All the bending happens on the way out, at the two slanted faces, and that is where the colours come apart, sorted by how much each one deviates.

ColourDeviationWhere it goes
RedLeastCarries straight on, out through the apex
GreenMoreBends away to the left
BlueMostBends away to the right

So a prism whose apex points east takes white light travelling east, sends red on east, green north and blue south. Because the prism is fixed, those three exits are fixed too, which tells you a great deal before you touch anything: you already know which direction each colour will set off in, and therefore roughly where its gem has to be reached from.

Solve red first

Red is the beam that carries straight on, so it is the one whose path you can read at a glance. Green and blue both come off at right angles, which takes a moment longer to picture. Getting red home first also settles the direction white is entering by, which pins down where green and blue are starting from. Working in that order turns one hard three-way problem into one decision followed by two simpler ones.

Read the stones

Every gem is cut from the colour it is waiting for. A dark ruby wants red, a dark sapphire wants blue, and they sit unlit until that light actually arrives. Three of them want a colour you cannot get from the prism directly, and those are the ones that make a board hard:

  • An amber stone wants yellow, which is red and green together.
  • A violet-pink stone wants magenta, which is red and blue.
  • A pale blue-green stone wants cyan, which is green and blue.

An unlit gem also shows what it is currently receiving, tinted faintly across the crystal. That is worth checking before you touch anything: a stone that wants cyan and is already washed faintly green is telling you half the job is done and only the blue route is wrong.

Mixed gems are two puzzles that argue with each other

This is where Prism gets its teeth. A gem that wants a single primary needs one route to work. A gem that wants yellow needs two separate beams to land on the same square, and the mirrors that steer them are often shared. Flip one to bring red home and you can knock green off its path in the same move.

The way through is to stop treating them as one problem. Pick the beam with the longer or more awkward route and solve that one on its own, ignoring the other entirely. Then bring the second beam in to meet it, and only touch mirrors the first beam does not use. If you cannot do that, the mirror they share is the one that has to change, and both routes have to be rebuilt around its new setting.

When a beam sails past its gem

Look at the last mirror that beam actually touched, not the one nearest the gem. A beam misses its target because of where it was last turned, and the piece sitting beside the gem is frequently not on its path at all. Follow the light backwards from where it ends up to the last place it changed direction, and start there.

Not every mirror matters

From the third level on, some mirrors are decoys. In the solved board no beam ever touches them, so their setting is irrelevant to the answer. They are not there to waste your time so much as to catch light that has gone astray, which is genuinely useful information: if a beam is bouncing off a mirror standing on its own in an empty part of the board, that beam is somewhere it was never meant to be.

Use par as a hint, not a score

Par is the exact number of mirrors that were flipped out of true when the board was built, so it is the true minimum. If par is 4 and you have used nine flips, you have almost certainly fixed something and then unfixed it. That is the moment to Restart and go again with a plan, rather than keep patching. Restarting costs nothing and a board solved cleanly at par is a much better feeling than one bludgeoned in twenty.

Quick checklist

  • White into the prism first, and onto its flat face. Nothing else can be right before that.
  • A slanted face scatters the light and loses it. The prism has one working side.
  • Red continues straight out of the apex, green bends left, blue bends right.
  • Solve red, then green and blue.
  • Amber, violet-pink and blue-green stones need two beams arriving together.
  • For a mixed gem, finish the harder beam first, then bring the second one in.
  • If a beam misses, go back to the last mirror it touched.
  • Over par by a lot? Restart and plan rather than patch.
