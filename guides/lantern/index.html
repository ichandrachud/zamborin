Lantern strategy: the speed of your hand is the whole game

Lantern gives you one thing to do and one number to control. You hold a flame, the fireflies come to it, and they follow you at a speed of their own. Everything the game asks of you comes out of the gap between how fast you move and how fast they can keep up.

They follow the flame, they do not track your path

A firefly steers toward where the flame is now, not toward where it has been. On a straight run that looks the same. On a corner it is not the same at all: the flies behind you cut across the inside of the bend, because the inside is the shortest way to where the light has moved to.

That single fact is the whole game, because the webs are on the insides of the corners. Take a bend slowly and the line folds around it behind you. Take it quickly and the tail of the line swings wide into the silk.

The last one in the line is the one you lose

The fly nearest the flame is barely lagging. The one at the back is lagging the most, so it cuts the most and it is the one that touches a web. When you are judging a corner, judge it for the last firefly, not the first.

The practical version: come out of a bend wider than feels necessary, and give the tail a moment to straighten before the next one.

Read the corridor before you move

Every level is a corridor. The webs line both of its walls and it always runs from where the fireflies wake up to the jar, so a clear route is guaranteed to exist. The route is not hidden, it is simply drawn in the gaps rather than in the ink.

Look at the whole board before you press. The corridor narrows and gains another turn as the tiers rise, and the difference between an easy level and a hard one is almost entirely how many corners it makes you take.

You can see the silk. They cannot.

This is the asymmetry the game is built on. A firefly has no idea a web is there. You are the only one in the garden who knows what is coming, which is why leaving the swarm to drift is never a neutral choice. Every firefly lost to a web is lost to something you were shown and did not act on.

Go back for the one that gets caught

A fly that touches silk is held, not lost. A spider starts walking out along its web toward it, and its position is the clock: there are no numbers, just how much web is left between them. You have roughly twelve seconds.

Bring the flame close and hold it there and the fly strains toward the light hard enough to tear itself out. It takes about a second and a half of holding still. The cost is not the rescue, it is the time you are not spending with the rest of the line.

The jar holds fewer than the garden has

From tier five on there are spare fireflies, so one or two can be lost without ending the level. That margin is what a mistake spends. When the read-out says no spares, the next web ends the run, and that is the moment to stop hurrying.

Slow beats fast, and fast beats frantic

Measured across the tiers, a slow deliberate hand finishes more levels than a quick one and far more than a hurried one. But slow is not free either: loitering beside a web is its own risk, because the line jitters around the flame while you sit there. There is a pace in between, and finding it is the game.

The short version

  • Look at the whole corridor before you press.
  • Take corners wide, and judge them for the last firefly rather than the first.
  • Do not park next to silk. Moving slowly is not the same as standing still.
  • If one gets caught, go and get it. Twelve seconds is plenty if you start now.
  • When the spares are gone, slow down.
