Marble strategy: look before you roll

Marble has two halves. The course is a test of the hands: turns, gaps, traffic, wind, a sky train and a canyon of crystals, all high above a neon city. The square at the end of every course from level 2 is a test of the head: its way out is shut, and rolling faster will not open it. Most falls on the course come from moving too soon, and most wasted time in a square comes from the same thing.

Steer with small drags

The camera almost never turns. Drag up and the marble rolls away from you, drag down and it comes back, and that stays true on every level. The two exceptions are in the circus: its house of mirrors swings the view round while your drag stays as it was, to confuse you on purpose, and in its mirror maze the view turns with the marble, so dragging up still rolls the way you face. How far you drag is how hard it rolls, so a short drag is a gentle nudge and a long one is full power.

The commonest mistake is holding a long drag into a turn. The marble carries its speed, and a narrow plank does not forgive it. Ease off before a turn, not in it, and save the long drags for the straights. After a speed strip, ease off early: the rush lasts about a second after you leave the arrows.

A fall costs time, not progress

Roll through a blue ring and it turns green: that is where you start again. Fall off and the marble flies back to the last green ring. The fall is counted, and nothing else is lost. So on a hard stretch the calm thing is to go at it cleanly the first time, but the second try is never far away.

Everything that moves shows you first

Nothing on the course surprises you if you watch it once. A moving pad waits at each end of its run, so roll on while it waits. A see-through bridge flickers just before it goes dark. A crossing's light turns green when the flying cars leave a gap. Streaks of light and a rising whoosh come just before a gust of wind. A scanner bar sweeps a steady rhythm. Stop at the line and watch one cycle before you commit: it costs a second or two and saves a fall.

At a fork, the narrow way is quicker and the wide way is safer. Both lead on, so take the wide way until you know the level, and the narrow way when you are chasing its star.

The crystal canyon is slippery

A wormhole on the road takes the marble to the crystal canyon, and its green crystal road has less grip than the city's. Let go well before a turn: the marble keeps sliding. The canyon has its own dangers, each with a rhythm to read: crystals growing out of the road, fire jets that flare and die, fireballs that roll across on a green light, and crystal bridges that crack under you and fall away. On those bridges the rule reverses: never stop.

Tokyo's wormholes, the torii, lead to the same canyon. In the pinball machine a wormhole leads to the moon playfield instead: the same machine on a volcanic moon, just as slippery, where the fire jets are volcanic vents and the fireballs are lava bombs.

Puzzle squares: read the whole square first

As you roll up to a square the camera rises to show all of it. That is the moment to stop. Before you move, answer two questions: what does each gate need, and where is it? A gate's sign floats over it: the shape of a key, a letter, a bolt of lightning or a sun. Everything you need is in view, and nothing is hidden.

If you get it wrong, the reset pad on the bay beside the road in puts the whole square back as it was: keys, gates, crates, bridges, charge and mirrors. It is there to be used.

Keys: count before you open

A gate opens for the key of its colour and shape, and it keeps the key. You carry one key at a time: take another and the one you held is left on that stand. So a key is a thing you spend. Count the gates of each colour and the keys of that colour before you open anything. When there are more gates than keys, one of those gates is meant to stay shut, and it is usually the one closest to hand.

Switches: every gate of that letter flips

A switch flips every gate with its letter: shut gates open and open gates shut. The trap is the gate you are not looking at. The switch that opens the room ahead can shut the corridor behind you, or the room the switch itself stands in. Work out where you will be standing after each press, and whether you can still get back to the switch you need next. Pressing a switch twice puts its gates back, so a switch is never a dead end on its own.

Crates: think in pushes

A plate holds its gates open while something heavy sits on it. Roll into a crate, square on to one of its sides, and it slides one cell. A crate cannot be pulled and never passes through a gate, so a crate pushed against a wall can only move along that wall, and a crate in a corner is stuck for good. Before each push, ask where you will need to stand for the next one. When two crates are involved, the one in the way usually has to move first.

Turning bridges: count quarter turns

A lever turns every bridge with its letter a quarter turn clockwise. A bridge is open only where its lit road meets the side; railings close the rest. Three quarter turns clockwise are one quarter turn back, so a lever always gets you where you want to go if you count. The subtle part is where you are standing: a turn that joins the next road can cut off the one you came in on, and that is sometimes exactly what you need.

Charge: like charges push apart

A bolt pad charges the marble, and it crackles yellow; a ground pad empties it. Lightning gates let only a charged marble through. Floors marked with plus signs push a charged marble away, and their field stands up while you are charged, so you can see it. That gives every route a sign: some stretches need you charged and some need you empty. Never ground yourself in a room that only charge can leave, unless it has another way out.

Light: every mirror turns every time

A lamp shines a beam across the square, over the walls. Rolling under a mirror turns it a quarter turn, and a gate of light is open only while the beam reaches the crystal. Trace the beam from the lamp before you turn anything. Then look at your route out: rolling under a mirror on the way out turns it again, and the gate shuts in front of you. Rolling back over it once more turns it back.

On the road from level 41

From level 41 two more puzzles stand on the road in every level, before the square at the finish. There are eight kinds, and each comes back harder every four levels. Each one says what it wants the first time you meet it.

  • Ice mazes. On ice the marble slides until something stops it: a rock stops it a cell short, snow stops it on the snow, a wall stops it too, and a hole drops it. Plan the whole way out before the first push.
  • Build a road. A crate pushed into a gap fills it, and then you can roll across. Count the gaps against the crates, and push the far ones first.
  • Every tile. Each tile lights as you roll onto it and crumbles when you leave. Usually only one way covers them all, so work out where it has to end.
  • The tune. Listen to the drums, then roll over them in the same order, going round the others. The pad by the road plays it again.
  • The twin. Your twin moves the mirror way. A wall stops one of you and not the other, and that is how you get out of step.
  • The boat. Fill the channel exactly to the line: the dots on a tank are how much it holds. Roll over the wrong valve and the pad by the road empties it all.
  • The planks. Tap to hop to the next lane. Watch the lane ahead, and hop before your plank sinks at the end.
  • The riddle. Find the rule in the picture: shape, colour, and later how many. Roll onto the tile that belongs. A wrong one falls away.

The pinball machine, from level 101

Levels 101 to 150 run through a pinball machine with a space theme. It starts gentler than the end of Tokyo and climbs again, and every level has three puzzles of its own, five kinds in turn, each harder every time it comes.

  • The constellation. Rolling onto a star flips it and every star joined to it by a line. Order does not matter, and rolling over a star twice undoes it, so work out which stars to roll over, not when. Start from the stars at the ends of lines.
  • Refuel and launch. Each fuel cell fills a third of the tank, and the tank leaks. The launch pad throws you over the gap only with the tank over the line, so the cells that count are the last three you take: fill up close to the pad.
  • Wormholes. A scoop sends you to the lit exit of its colour, wherever it is. A flag swaps which exit of its colour is lit. Work backwards from the island with the way out: which colour lands there, and which flag lights it?
  • Satellites. A pushed satellite glides until a wall, a rock or another satellite stops it, and one that stops on a dock stays there. Use one satellite to stop another. A satellite in a corner can never move again: the pad by the road puts them back.
  • The airlock. The keys flash a code; roll over them in the same order, going round the others. A lamp over the door lights for each digit you have right.

The machine brings its own obstacles too. Ride round a black hole's rim, never across it. Go through docking clamps just after they open. Pass a magnet while it is dark. Slip past each droid on the side it is not guarding. Under the volcano, watch for the glowing rings and keep out of them.

The circus, from level 151

Levels 151 to 200 run round a tin toy circus. It starts gentler again and climbs to 200, and every level has three circus puzzles, seven kinds in turn, each harder every time it comes.

  • The clown car. Clowns get in only through the car's back door, where the white arrows point in; every other side is a wall. Work out which side each clown must be pushed from before you push it, because a clown against a wall cannot come back.
  • The acrobat pyramid. Push an acrobat onto every gold star. Fill the stars furthest in first, so the ones you fill do not block the rest.
  • The balance scales. The boards over the pans add up the kilos on each side, an anvil's too. You will not need every weight: find the ones that make both sides the same before you push, and check you can still get to the way out.
  • The shell game. Keep your eye on the cup with the gold star, not on the magician's hands. The wrong cup sends you back to watch again, so do not guess.
  • The knife thrower. The boards he hits are the ones to avoid; the ones he misses make a path. Remember the path, not the knives.
  • Ticket turnstiles. There are never enough tickets for every turnstile. Work out which turnstiles lead to more tickets, and pay for those first. The way out takes tickets too.
  • The house of mirrors. You see only round the marble. Try each opening in turn and remember where you have been; glass you bump into stays cracked, so you know it next time.

The circus brings its acts too. Roll through a ring of fire's middle. Pass the juggler just after a spot's ball has gone up. Reach the knife wheel as a hole comes to the bottom. Roll into the cannon as it points at the net. Ride the trapeze from edge to edge. The Ferris wheel stops with a car level at each end of the broken rail: roll on while it stands still, and roll off when your car stops beside the far rail. Wait on the yellow spots of the teeterboard and the Wheel of Death. Outrun the clown car.

The circus's magician's cabinets lead into a maze of mirrors, a different one in each level and bigger toward 200. Every mirror shows another corridor, so a dead end looks like a way on, and some doorways are clear glass that only looks open. Bump a pane and it cracks, so you know it next time. Inside, the view comes down behind the marble and turns with it; dragging up still rolls the way you face. Keep one wall on the same side of the marble and follow it: in every one of these mazes that leads to the cabinet at the far end, the way out.

Stars

Each level has a star time, shown beside the star at the bottom of the screen. It was set by a quick, clean run that knew the square's answer, so a star is for a level you have already worked out. Take the narrow way at forks, keep your speed on the straights, and in the square go straight to the answer.

The short version

  • Ease off before turns. Long drags are for straights.
  • Watch one cycle of anything that moves before you commit.
  • At a square, stop and read it all: what each gate needs and where it is.
  • Count keys against gates; a gate keeps its key.
  • Know where you will stand after each press, push or turn.
  • Every pad works each time you roll onto it, on the way out too.
  • Use the reset pad when a square has gone wrong.
