Carrom strategy: how to aim the striker, pot your discs, and take the Queen

Carrom rewards a soft touch far more than a big flick. Here's everything that actually matters — tuned to how Carrom plays on Zamborin, where you flick the blue striker from the bottom base line and race the computer to clear all nine of your discs.

Aiming and shooting — it's a slingshot

You play white and shoot from the bottom of the board; the computer plays black from the top. To fire, tap the striker and pull back, away from where you want it to go, then release — it launches in the opposite direction, like drawing back a slingshot or pulling a pool cue. So to send the striker up the board into the pack, you drag it down. A thin white line previews the exact path while you hold, so trust the line, not your instinct about "dragging toward" the target.

One hard rule the game enforces: the striker must travel away from your base line. A flick that would go sideways or back into your own edge simply won't fire, so there's no penalty for lining up a shot and then thinking better of it.

Slide the striker before you shoot

Before flicking, you can reposition the striker anywhere along your base line by dragging on the line itself (not on the striker). That slide covers most of the width of the board, and it is the single most underused tool for beginners. Pick your spot on the line first, then worry about power. Moving the striker a few centimetres left or right changes the angle into a disc completely — often the difference between a clean pot and a rattle. The game also won't let you park the striker directly on top of a disc sitting on your line; it nudges you clear automatically.

Power and angle control

Power is set by how far you pull back — and full power arrives fast, at a drag of only about a quarter of the board. That means most of your useful shots live in the first half of the drag. A short, controlled pull is a delicate cut; you rarely need to yank the striker to the far edge of the screen. Because there's real friction, aim to hit your disc with just enough pace to reach the pocket and stop — not enough to send it caroming off three cushions and back into open play.

For angle, remember the striker pushes a disc along the line joining their two centres at the moment of contact. To pot a disc into a corner, aim the striker at the point on the disc that sits directly opposite the pocket — the "ghost" contact point — not at the disc's middle. Nudging the striker along the base line is how you set up that contact point without changing your power.

The break

Your first shot smashes the central flower of nineteen discs. Don't waste it on raw power. A dead-centre cannonball scatters everything evenly and usually pots nothing. Instead, aim slightly off-centre with medium power so the striker glances into the pack and peels a few discs toward a corner, while keeping the striker itself out of a pocket. A good break leaves you two or three of your own discs sitting near pockets with clean lines — that's worth far more than a spectacular scatter.

Keep your turn going

Pot one of your own discs (white) and you shoot again — that's how runs are built. But your turn ends the instant you miss, sink one of the opponent's discs, or commit a foul by pocketing the striker itself. So every shot is really two questions: can I pot a disc, and will the striker stay safe afterward? A tap that pots a disc and dies in open space beats a heavy shot that pots the disc but follows it into the corner.

Use the cushions

The rails behave like a real board — discs and the striker rebound off them at a slightly reduced speed. When a disc is tucked where you can't hit it straight, bank the striker off a side cushion to come at it from a new angle. Cushion shots are also the safe way to attack a disc parked near your own base line, which you can't hit going forward. Plan the bounce with the same ghost-contact-point thinking — just mirror your aim across the wall.

Going for the Queen

The single red disc in the centre is the Queen, and the game treats her with the traditional cover rule. You can't legally take her until you've already pocketed at least one of your own discs — sink her before that and she's returned to the centre. Once you're eligible, you must cover her: pocket one of your own discs in the same shot as the Queen, or on your very next shot. Miss the cover and she goes straight back to the middle. So don't chase the Queen early or in isolation — line her up only when you've got an easy own-disc pot to seal the deal right behind her.

Difficulty levels

  • Easy — the computer picks loosely from all its options and shoots with a lot of aim wobble. Great for learning the physics and finishing boards you're winning.
  • Medium — it plays a steady positional game, choosing from its top few disc-to-pocket lines with modest error. It'll punish you if you gift it easy turns.
  • Hard — it takes its single best line every time with almost no wobble, and it rarely wastes a turn. Against Hard, safety matters as much as potting: don't leave the striker sitting on an obvious line for it.

You can play a single board or a best-of-three match, where the first side to win two boards takes it. A board is won by whoever clears all nine of their colour first.

Quick checklist

  • Pull the striker back away from the target — it's a slingshot; watch the white line.
  • Slide the striker along your base line to set the angle before touching the power.
  • Full power comes early in the drag — favour soft, controlled taps.
  • Aim at the disc's ghost point opposite the pocket, not its centre.
  • Pot your own colour to keep shooting; never follow a disc into the pocket.
  • Take the Queen only when an own-disc pot is ready to cover her.

Put it into practice against the computer at three difficulty levels.
